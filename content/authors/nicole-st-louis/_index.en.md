## Research project

The most decisive factor in a star’s life is its mass. The most massive stars live for only a few million years but nevertheless have a significant impact on their surrounding interstellar medium (ISM). These bright, hot stars form deep within molecular clouds and, even before they are fully formed, develop a strong stellar wind that persists throughout all their evolutionary phases. Through these outflows, they chemically enrich the gas and transfer a large amount of energy and momentum to it. This leaves information-rich traces in the ISM, in the form of ionised nebulae or gas bubbles, which provide insights into the evolutionary stages the star went through before ending its life in a supernova explosion. These winds therefore enable massive stars to play a decisive role in the evolution of gas, dust and, ultimately, the stellar populations that make up galaxies. Nicole St-Louis’s research focuses primarily on Wolf-Rayet (WR) stars, which burn helium in their cores after having exhausted their hydrogen supply, and more specifically on large-scale structures found in the wind of around 20 per cent of these stars, known as co-rotating interaction regions (CIRs). These form when a disturbance at the base of the wind generates a flow with a velocity different from that of the ambient wind; as the star rotates, this flow collides with the wind, thereby creating spiral-shaped regions with densities higher and lower than those of the surrounding medium. The source of the disturbance remains unknown, but magnetic fields and pulsations are viable candidates. Very little is known about these physical processes in massive stars, and particularly in WR stars, as the fact that their surfaces remain buried beneath the dense wind greatly complicates their study. The main objective is to characterise the properties of RICs and to seek links with the star’s physical parameters in order to understand the origin of the disturbances that produce them, thereby enhancing our understanding of the physical parameters of massive stars. Nicole St-Louis also uses the Fourier transform imaging spectrograph, SITELLE, installed on the Canada-France-Hawaii Telescope, to observe the circumstellar environment of a sample of massive stars at various evolutionary stages with unprecedented spatial and spectral resolution.  This state-of-the-art instrument enables us to determine the characteristics of the material ejected by the star during its lifetime, such as its enrichment in heavy elements, its asymmetries and kinematics, as well as its density and temperature structures. By comparing these observations with the predictions of theoretical models, we can study the details of the evolutionary sequence of massive stars, from their formation right up to the phase immediately preceding the supernova explosion—a sequence of which many aspects still elude us today.
