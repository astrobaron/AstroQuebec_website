## Research project

The main research work of David Lafrenière and his group focuses on the study of exoplanets and aims to establish the prevalence and diversity of planetary systems in our galaxy, to understand how these systems form and evolve, to gain a thorough understanding of the physics at play in their atmospheres and interiors, and ultimately, to detect signs of life elsewhere in the Universe. David Lafrenière and his research group are primarily interested in characterising the atmospheres of exoplanets using transit and eclipse spectroscopy, notably with the NIRISS instrument on the James Webb Space Telescope and with infrared échelle spectrographs on ground-based telescopes. Using the SPIRou and NIRPS instruments, his group searches for new rocky planets orbiting M-type stars using radial velocity measurements.  Furthermore, his group uses direct imaging techniques at infrared wavelengths to detect new planets and then measure their physical properties. To be able to ‘see’ these very faint planets, which orbit very close to their star—which is several million times brighter—it is necessary to develop new observation and image-processing techniques and even to build new instruments. At present, current technology makes it possible to detect giant gas planets with orbits the size of those of the outer planets in the Solar System, or larger.
